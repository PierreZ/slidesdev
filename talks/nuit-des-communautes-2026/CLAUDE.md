## Narrative spine: "My job was never typing, it was finding out I was wrong before the users did. What makes a project survive agents is the harness (Rust, tests, simulation), and now that code costs nothing we finally have time to build it. The niche becomes the standard because you cannot automate a factory without an inspector. Same job, new era, and the era is a lot more interesting."

Chronological, first person, optimistic, told as a career story from 2010 to today. Pierre's emphasis, added 12 September 2026 after a full re-read: the new software era is more interesting, niche techniques become standard, and that is the prerequisite for automating the factory. "Niche" is planted on the FoundationDB and Materia slides and paid on the ladder and the closing slide. No cold-open war story: the Hadoop turtle arrives as the 2015 beat, where it happened. Storytelling over numbers: figures appear only as punchlines (800 servers, 250 machines, 4,000 years, ten minutes, four days, one week, three months, two thirds, 130 evenings).

**FinistDevs (slide 1), then the cover (slide 2) and whoami (slide 3).** The deck opens on the community because it is the Nuit des Communautés and Pierre speaks for it: FinistJUG since December 2011, 130+ evenings, three organizers, next evening placeholder.

**Act 1, the loop through my career (slides 4 to 10), then Materia (slides 11 and 12).** 2010, how I started coding: engineering school in Brest, C and many other things, then FinistDevs and a lot more technologies, part-time internships always in infrastructure teams (Pierre's own three bullets), motif state 1 (segfault). Why distributed systems: one line only at Pierre's request, "because they are hard, there is a lot to learn from them", plus a meme placeholder (`public/distsys-meme.png`). 2017, the pager (full time at OVH Metrics since 2016, on call from late 2017, Pierre's dates): motif state 2, the OVH Metrics numbers, the "this is fine" flame gif (Pierre: "add a flame meme, I will just talk about it"), and the thesis: my job became finding out I'm wrong before the users do. The Hadoop turtle is his to tell verbally here. Then a link slide to his Devoxx France talk "Développer des applications observables pour la production" (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ivnI1BKywW4&t=603s, YouTube thumbnail), which stands in for the on-call lessons he did not want to write. Then the wall of papers reused from the Sunny Tech deck ("the world is worse than your tests", nine beliefs, nine papers), ending on the question "can we automate finding what we don't know in the code?". Then "Down the rabbit hole of correctness" (Pierre's ordering: stay on the distributed systems track): some software cannot afford the pager (planes, pacemakers, databases), those teams built stronger harnesses, and Klabnik's list of sixty-year-old niche techniques (types, property-based testing, fuzzing, model checking, deterministic simulation, proofs), "distributed systems live down that hole, that is where I went". HBase (250+ machines, the repair tool that moves data out, the pager tells the truth too late) and FoundationDB (the simulator written before the database, "a handful of companies in the world work this way, the niche inside the niche") share the motif state 3 slide, "same move every job: compiler, pager, simulator". Materia, two slides, added because Pierre felt Claude and squash came too early: "The simulator became the tool" (1 to 6 people, six people at a French cloud provider doing what Apple does, we cheated by running our code inside FoundationDB's simulator, every commit through partitions and crashes, the newcomer in a week, "a simulator is not a test, it is the tool we write complicated software with"), then the Materia TUI screenshot. Pierre asked for this to stay very short: the point is that the simulator became a tool for writing complicated software, not a test.

**Act 2, the LLM part, same shape as act 1 (slides 13 to 19).** Incident: "Then the LLMs showed up" (centered, skeptic sentence placeholder), then "Same company, same model, not the same code": 70 developers at Clever Cloud got the same agents on the same day, quality varied wildly, three kinds of projects came out AI-approved (written in Rust, with lots of tests, under a simulator), "not the model, not the prompt, the harness". Diagnosis: "So, what went wrong?" carries Klabnik's "AI broke all three" (understood, wrote, tried, only the pager is left) and closes on "the three kinds of projects that held up had already replaced those three, they had a harness". Answer: "You don't trust Claude, you trust the harness", Boris Cherny's tweet (give Claude a way to verify its work) with the line "the more constraints you give it, the better the output"; then "Simulation finds unknown unknowns", the BugBash arc and team adoption, the simulator as the strongest constraint. What it gives: "I went to squash" (night shift, four days, code disposable, invariants and seeds survive). Pivot, centered: "So, what changed?", we never had time for software quality, now that code costs almost nothing the time is back and goes into specs, simulation, invariants. The motif has three states on screen (segfault, pager, simulator); the fourth (Claude as writer) is spoken. Squash and the four-day Materia rewrite share a slide: the night shift has a new worker, and years of tests and simulation checked every step, code is cheap, the loop is the capital.

**Act 3, the factory (slides 20 to 26).** Factorio image: I used to craft everything by hand, now I build small factories. "A factory needs its own inspector": in a workshop I inspect every gear, in a factory the line rejects the bad gear by itself, the loop is the inspector (the cheapest one being the language that says no the fastest, which absorbed the old "pick the language" slide), without it an agent is a faster typist and I am the bottleneck, "you cannot automate a factory that has no inspector, nothing new, the pager taught me that in 2017". This is the prerequisite claim. Then the skeptic as a quoted title, "Tu deviens testeur, c'est cher payé" (his CEO, 2026), answered by what he actually does all day, the slide Pierre asked for explicitly ("all the work I find interesting is still there", inspired by Kiro's architect-not-typist without citing it): decide what done means, build the harness that says wrong to whoever types, check the output is the intent (review, debug, replay seeds), ask dumb questions, learn a domain in an afternoon, try three prototypes and keep one; "I hand-write less than 1% of the code, I still make 100% of the decisions, why would you lower the bar for yourself?" (the last line restates a post Pierre likes, unattributed). The Kiro principles are not cited on any slide: fast feedback loop lives on the inspector slide, trust the boundaries on the Boris slide, code disposable but boundary tests not on the squash slide, human standards on "You own what ships". Proof of reach: paros, then FoundationDB in C++. "You own what ships under your name": throwaway code as black box, agent code in production needs a higher bar, if you can't debug it you can't own it, two thirds of assumptions wrong on hard problems. "Reserved for Google. Built on my evenings": paros and the bug never looked for, reframed as reach (what was out of reach is now one person and a few evenings away). FoundationDB in C++ without C++ and the PR the simulator kept red. Not a silver bullet.

**Close (slides 27 to 30).** Bookend to the pager ("Remember the pager?", the turtle being spoken only): that combination is a seed, found at night while I play squash. "The niche is the new standard": the five-level ladder, every level a niche technique now standard for anyone running agents. "Same job, new era": coffee without the night shift, reading diffs and seeds, writing what must always be true, building the loop, the hard part is all that is left, "the niche I lived in became the standard, my job did not change, the era did, and it got a lot more interesting". Thanks, with a FinistDevs callback.
